Method overview.

A target speedup determines how many denoising steps can reuse cached computation. MORCA learns where to use them.

Abstract

Diffusion Transformers (DiTs) have achieved remarkable performance in video synthesis, but their iterative denoising process suffers from high inference latency. Caching has emerged as an effective acceleration strategy by exploiting inter-step redundancy during denoising. Existing dynamic caching methods typically estimate the error that cache reuse would introduce at each denoising step (step error) to guide cache decisions, whereas our concern is how much fidelity loss cache reuse would cause in the final generated video (terminal error). We show that step error does not directly correspond to terminal error and latent information helps capture their relationship, thereby informing cache decisions. Moreover, these methods rely on threshold-based rules that do not provide precise speedup control, making it difficult to meet user-specified acceleration targets. To address these limitations, we introduce MORCA, a cache scheduling framework trained through offline-to-online reinforcement learning to make latent-aware reuse/recompute decisions under user-specified acceleration targets. Extensive experiments on different video generation models across multiple speedup targets demonstrate that MORCA achieves better speed–fidelity trade-offs than state-of-the-art caching methods.

What does one cache decision cost?

Step error measures the immediate difference between a cached and a recomputed residual. Terminal error measures the effect on the final video against a no-cache reference. On Wan2.1, two prompts with similar step-error curves can end up with different final-video fidelity.

Adding features from the current and previous latents improves the predictor’s estimate of final-video PSNR.

Latent-aware, budget-constrained scheduling

The LABC scheduler combines SeaCache’s step-error signals with features of the current, previous, and last recomputed latents. It uses this state to decide whether to reuse the cached residual at each step.

Calibration converts the requested speedup into an integer reuse budget. The scheduler tracks how many reuses it has spent and allows only actions that can still meet the budget by the final step.

Offline-to-online optimization

Offline implicit Q-learning (IQL) starts from trajectories generated with random schedules. Online fine-tuning then adds new policy rollouts to the replay data. The training reward is the final video’s capped PSNR relative to an uncached reference with the same prompt and seed.
